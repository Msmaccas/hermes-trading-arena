# Buffet — Deep Research Entry
## Week of 2026-06-08

## MARKET SENTIMENT ASSESSMENT

Well, let me tell you what I see when I look at this week's market. Mr. Market is having one of his more dramatic mood swings. The Nasdaq 100 fell 4.80% in a single week. The semiconductor index — down 9.22%. That's a haircut that would make a barber blush. And what did the safe harbors do? Consumer Staples (XLP) up 1.71%. Healthcare (XLV) up 0.61%. Utilities (XLU) up 0.93%. The flight from hot money into dependable cash flows is in full swing.

Now, I've seen this movie before. I've seen it in 1973–74, when the Nifty Fifty collapsed. I saw it in 1987, when the Dow dropped 22% in a single day. I saw it in 2000, when the tech bubble burst and the Nasdaq lost 78% of its value. I saw it in 2008, when the entire financial system teetered. And you know what? In every single case, the businesses that had durable competitive advantages — the ones with pricing power, low capital intensity, and predictable demand — they came through just fine. The stocks recovered, and so did the companies' earnings.

As I wrote in my 2013 letter to Berkshire shareholders: "The business underlying the stock — which is what we own — has wonderful economics and a durable competitive advantage. That's what matters." Not the daily price quote. Not the week's performance. Not the macro noise.

Let me assess the major markets individually.

**United States:** The S&P 500 fell 2.58%, the Dow 1.35%, the Russell 2000 3.55%. The breadth is terrible — technology and semiconductors getting crushed. But financials (XLF +0.21%) and real estate (XLRE +0.68%) held up. This is a rotation, not a crash. The money is moving from the speculative frenzy of AI stocks into businesses that actually generate cash and pay dividends. That's not a bad thing. That's Mr. Market doing what he does — transferring money from the impatient to the patient.

**China:** The Shanghai market had a few speculative pops — up 20% on some small-cap names. But these are not businesses I can understand. I've said many times: "You don't have to be an expert on every company. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence." Beijing Deep Glint Technology? Orbbec? I couldn't tell you how those businesses make money in five minutes, so I stay out.

**Hong Kong:** Shandong Molong Petroleum up 29% on high volume. Kingboard Holdings up 12%. Again, outside my circle. I've never owned a Hong Kong listed company in my life, and I'm not about to start now.

**India:** NRB Bearings up 15%. A bearing manufacturer? That's a commodity business. No brand, no pricing power, no moat. As I said in the 2007 letter: "A business with a commodity product is a terrible business to be in. The only way to make money is to be the low-cost producer. And even then, the returns are mediocre."

**Japan:** VALUENEX Japan up 19.6% — a software company. FDK Corporation up 11.5% — batteries. These are small, speculative names. Not my game.

**UK:** Raspberry PI Holdings up 27.6% — a single-board computer company. Tate & Lyle up 16% on acquisition rumors. Tate & Lyle is interesting — a food ingredients company with real brands. But an acquisition at $5 billion changes the calculus. More on that later.

**Brazil:** Mostly ADRs of US companies — Walmart, Eli Lilly, Bank of America. Nothing new.

**Korea:** SK Networks up 30%, I-Robotics up 30%, PAMTEK up 30%. These are speculative micro-caps. No moat, no earnings visibility. I'd be a buyer of none.

So what's the market telling us? It's telling us that the easy money in technology is over, for now. The Federal Reserve is not cutting rates — the jobs report was strong, and inflation is sticky. The weak hands are selling. And the smart money is slowly rotating into businesses that have been ignored: consumer staples, healthcare, utilities.

As I wrote in my 1997 letter: "The most important quality for an investor is temperament, not intellect." Right now, temperament means not panicking. It means staying grounded in what you know. It means looking at your watchlist and asking: which of these businesses will generate more cash five years from now than they do today?

Let me walk through my watchlist, stock by stock.

## WATCHLIST ANALYSIS

### PICK: TXRH (Texas Roadhouse)

**Price:** $170.46 | **Market Cap:** $11.20B | **PE:** 27.19 | **EPS:** $6.27
**52w Range:** $153.83 – $197.00 | **Avg Vol:** 1.11M | **Today Vol:** 1.40M
**Recent News:** Q1 earnings beat, traffic growth focus, positive analyst questions.

Now, Texas Roadhouse. This is a business I can understand. You walk in, you order a steak, you pay your bill. Simple. Predictable. People eat steak in good times and bad — maybe less filet mignon in bad times, but they still show up for a $20 sirloin.

Let me run it through the four gates.

**Gate 1: Circle of Competence.** Restaurants are inside my circle. I bought Dairy Queen. I bought See's Candy. I understand the economics of putting food in front of people and collecting a profit. Texas Roadhouse is a casual dining chain with a strong brand. The model is simple: they serve consistent quality at fair prices, and they train their employees well. I can explain this business in under a minute.

**Gate 2: Moat.** The moat here is brand and operating culture. Texas Roadhouse has a loyal following — especially in the Southeast and Midwest. They've built a reputation for hand-cut steaks and fresh rolls. That's an intangible asset. It's not Coca-Cola strong, but it's real. They also have a cost advantage: they source beef efficiently and run high volumes per store. The average unit volume is north of $6 million. That gives them pricing power — they can raise prices a few percent without losing traffic. As I said in the 2011 letter: "The single most important decision in evaluating a business is pricing power. If you've got the power to raise prices without losing business to a competitor, you've got a great business."

**Gate 3: Owner Earnings.** Let's do the math. Net income: $6.27 per share, times shares outstanding — about 65.6 million shares gives net income around $412 million. Depreciation & amortization: I'd estimate around $130 million. Maintenance capex: restaurants need upkeep — maybe $90 million. So owner earnings = $412M + $130M – $90M = $452 million. That's about $6.89 per share. Free cash flow reported is $190.24 million for the year. The difference likely includes growth capex (new stores). The company is opening about 25–30 new stores per year. That's good — they're reinvesting at high returns. ROE is 28.9% — excellent. Profit margin 6.8% — decent for restaurants. Debt/Equity 68.53% — manageable. They have $284 million in free cash flow after growth capex. That's real cash.

What about intrinsic value? If owner earnings grow at 8% annually (reasonable given historical revenue growth of 12.8% and earnings growth of 10%), and I use a 5% discount rate (current long-term Treasury yield), the value of the business is about $20 billion — almost double the current market cap of $11.2 billion. Even with a conservative 6% growth rate, it's worth $15 billion. The stock at $170 is trading at about 27 times trailing earnings — not cheap, but not crazy for a 28% ROE business with pricing power.

**Gate 4: Five-Year Test.** Would I be happy owning Texas Roadhouse if the stock market closed for five years? Yes. The restaurants would still be serving steak. The brand would still be strong. They'd probably be opening 150 new stores over five years. The cash flow would pile up. I'd be fine.

**Risk management:** The restaurant industry is competitive. If the economy really tanks, traffic could dip. But Texas Roadhouse has weathered 2008, the pandemic, and inflation. They know how to manage. I would not buy at $197 (the 52-week high), but at $170 — down 14% from the high — it's getting interesting. I'd look to add on any further weakness below $160, my margin of safety zone.

**Verdict:** A wonderful business at a fair price. I'd buy a small position here and add if it dips.

The moat is switching costs — once you're fitted for a specific lens, you rarely switch. Also, the FDA regulatory barrier is huge. Cooper has two strong businesses: CooperVision (contact lenses) and CooperSurgical (fertility and women's health).

**Gate 1: Circle of Competence.** Medical supplies are inside my circle. I've owned Johnson & Johnson. I understand that recurring demand from doctors and patients creates predictable cash flows. Contact lenses are a duopoly with Alcon — high barriers to entry.

**Gate 2: Moat.** High switching costs for consumers (lens fit), regulatory barriers, and brand reputation. The contact lens market grows at 3-5% per year, and Cooper gains share. They also have a strong position in specialty lenses (toric, multifocal) which have higher margins.

**Gate 3: Owner Earnings.** The GAAP numbers are ugly — a net loss of $77.9 million due to a litigation charge. But that's a one-time event. Look at operating cash flow. Revenue is $1.08B per quarter, annualizing to $4.3B. Free cash flow is $424.28 million. Debt/Equity is 33% — very manageable. ROE is low at 2.9%, but that's because of the litigation charge — normalized ROE is probably 12-15%. The forward PE is 13.47x — that's cheap for a company with recurring revenue and pricing power.

**Gate 4: Five-Year Test.** Would I be happy owning Cooper if the market closed for five years? Yes. People will still need contact lenses. The surgical business (fertility) has secular tailwinds. The record sales of $1.08B show the underlying business is strong. The litigation is a cloud, but Cooper has the balance sheet to handle it.

**Risk management:** The litigation could be a persistent drag. But at $67.34, the stock is 25% off its 52-week high and trading at the low end of analyst targets ($66-$95). Volume is almost 3x average — big money is coming in. I'd buy here with a stop if it breaks below $58.

**Verdict:** A quality business at a discounted price due to a transitory issue. I'd buy.

### PICK: ABM (ABM Industries)

**Price:** $42.54 | **Market Cap:** $2.49B | **PE:** 16.36 | **EPS:** $2.60
**52w Range:** $36.96 – $52.06 | **Avg Vol:** 0.55M | **Today Vol:** 1.39M
**Recent News:** Q2 earnings beat, record first-half new sales bookings, maintained full-year outlook.

ABM provides facility services — janitorial, parking, engineering, HVAC. This is a boring business. Which is exactly why I like it. As I said in the 1992 letter: "We prefer businesses that are dull and mundane — ones that earn a predictable 15% on equity year after year."

**Gate 1: Circle of Competence.** Facility services is simple. Companies need their buildings cleaned and maintained. They sign contracts. The cash flow is recurring. I understand this.

**Gate 2: Moat.** ABM's moat is scale and long-term customer relationships. They are one of the largest facility services companies in the US. They have 100,000+ employees. To compete, a small firm would need to undercut on price — which destroys margins. ABM has pricing power because switching costs for a large client (e.g., a Fortune 500 HQ) are high — you don't want to train new janitors every year. They also have a government contracts business, which is sticky.

**Gate 3: Owner Earnings.** Net income: $2.60 per share on about 58.5 million shares = $152 million. Depreciation & amortization: I'd estimate $150 million. Maintenance capex: $60 million. Owner earnings = $152M + $150M – $60M = $242 million. That's about $4.14 per share. The stock at $42.54 is trading at about 10x owner earnings. That's cheap. Free cash flow is $284 million — even higher. ROE is 8.9%, which is mediocre, but the company carries some debt (D/E 112%). Still, the forward PE is 9.8x — extremely cheap for a business with 8.4% revenue growth and 9% earnings growth.

**Gate 4: Five-Year Test.** Would I be happy owning ABM if the market closed? Yes. Facilities don't go away. They sign long-term contracts. The cash flow is steady and growing. Even in a recession, maintenance doesn't stop entirely.

**Risk management:** The high debt/equity is a concern, but the cash flow covers interest easily. The stock is up 6.67% today on strong volume after earnings — smart money is buying. I'd buy at $42.54 and hold.

**Verdict:** A boring, predictable cash machine at a cheap price. I'm a buyer.

### PICK: FDXF (FedEx Freight Holding Company, Inc.)

**Price:** $167.84 | **Market Cap:** $24.99B | **PE:** 36.89 | **EPS:** $4.55
**52w Range:** $135.00 – $200.00 | **Avg Vol:** 2.76M | **Today Vol:** 2.68M

FedEx Freight is the less-than-truckload (LTL) division of FedEx, recently spun off. LTL shipping is a good business — it has network effects. The more hubs you have, the more routes you can offer, the more customers you attract.

**Gate 1: Circle of Competence.** I've owned airlines, railroads, and package delivery companies (UPS used to be a large Berkshire holding). I understand the economics of transportation networks.

**Gate 2: Moat.** LTL has high barriers: building a national network of terminals takes billions of dollars and years. FedEx Freight is one of the top two players with FedEx Ground. The moat is scale and infrastructure. As I wrote in the 2015 letter about BNSF: "A transportation network that is irreplaceable and earns decent returns on capital is a fine business."

**Gate 3: Owner Earnings.** PE is 37x trailing — looks expensive. But this is a newly independent company, so historical GAAP earnings may not reflect true earning power. Forward PE is 34.56x — still not cheap. Revenue is declining 4.7% year over year — that's a concern. However, profit margin is 10.4%, which is strong for transportation. No dividend yet — they will likely start one once the spin-off stabilizes.

**Gate 4: Five-Year Test.** LTL shipping demand is tied to GDP. It's cyclical. I'd be cautious — if the economy slows, volumes drop. But FedEx Freight has pricing power because it's an oligopoly. I'd want to see a lower valuation before buying.

**Verdict:** A good business but not at this price. I'd wait for a pullback to around $140-150.

## FINAL MARKET CALL

So what's my overall positioning?

The market is in a correction, driven by technology overvaluation and a strong jobs report that reduces the chance of rate cuts. Mr. Market is having a hissy fit. But I remind myself: "Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy only when others are fearful." The fear is concentrated in tech. The greed is concentrated in consumer staples and healthcare — and that's not real greed, that's common sense.

My cash level at Berkshire right now is about $280 billion. I'm not compelled to buy everything today. But I see several opportunities:

1. **Texas Roadhouse** — a wonderful business with a strong brand, pricing power, and high ROE. At $170, it's 14% off its high. I'd buy a small position and add on any further weakness.
2. **Cooper Companies** — a quality medical supply business with a transitory litigation cloud. At $67, it's 25% off its high. The forward PE is 13.5x. I'd buy a meaningful position.
3. **ABM Industries** — a boring cash machine at 10x owner earnings. I'd buy.
4. **Johnson & Johnson** — a healthcare powerhouse at 18x forward earnings. I'd buy.
5. **Consumer Staples ETF (XLP)** — as a core defensive holding, I'd buy on dips below $80.
6. **Healthcare ETF (XLV)** — the sector is rotating into value. I'd buy.

What I am NOT doing:
- Buying tech or semiconductors. That's outside my circle of competence at current valuations.
- Buying FedEx Freight at 37x earnings.
- Buying Insperity — a negative earnings, high debt business.
- Buying Chinese or Indian micro-caps.

**Timeframe for these trades:** I buy for the long term. Not for next week, not for next quarter. As I said in the 1996 letter: "We have no idea what the stock market will do in the short run. We do have a very strong opinion about what the businesses we own will do in the long run."

**Risk factors:** The biggest risk is that inflation reignites and interest rates rise further. That would compress multiples across the board. But wonderful businesses with pricing power can pass on costs. The second risk is recession hurting consumer spending. But even in a recession, people still buy steak (just less often), still need contacts, still need facility services.

**My final advice:** Ignore the noise. Focus on the businesses. Buy when the price is below intrinsic value. Hold for decades. And never forget: "The stock market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient." I've been patient for 60 years. It has served me well.
