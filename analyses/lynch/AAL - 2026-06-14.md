## THE PEG RATIO: The Most Important Number You're Not Using

I invented the PEG ratio because I got tired of people telling me a stock was "cheap" just because it had a low P/E. A P/E of 10 on a stock that's shrinking 10% a year is expensive. A P/E of 30 on a stock that's growing 30% a year is cheap.

The formula is simple: PEG = P/E ÷ Growth Rate. I look for PEG ratios under 1.5. Under 1.0 is ideal.

Now, for American Airlines, the P/E is 48.3. But what's the growth rate? That's where it gets tricky with cyclicals.

If we're at the bottom of the cycle, earnings could grow from 31 cents to $2 or $3 over the next few years. That would be growth of 500% to 1000%. But you can't use that number in the PEG ratio because it's not sustainable growth. That's a one-time recovery.

If we use a normalized growth rate — say 10% to 15% over a full cycle — then the PEG ratio is 48.3 ÷ 12.5 = 3.86. That's terrible. A PEG of 3.86 means the stock is priced for perfection.

But here's the real question: what's the sustainable growth rate for an airline? The answer is: very low. Airlines have historically grown earnings at 5% to 8% over long periods, because they're capital-intensive, union-heavy, and commodity-priced. If you use 7% growth, the PEG is 48.3 ÷ 7 = 6.9. That's off the charts.

Let me tell you what I told every investor who ever asked me about a high-PEG stock: "You're paying for growth that may never materialize." And with airlines, the growth is almost always cyclical, not secular.

## THE SIX CATEGORIES: Why American Airlines Fails Every One

Let me walk you through each of my six categories and explain why American Airlines doesn't fit any of them well.

**1. Fast Grower (20-25% growth)**

A fast grower needs room to expand into new markets. Think of McDonald's in the 1960s, adding restaurants at a 20% annual rate. Or La Quinta in the 1980s, building hotels in new cities.

American Airlines is not a fast grower. The airline industry is mature. There are only so many routes you can fly, and most of the good ones are already taken. The growth rate for the industry is 3% to 5% a year, tied to GDP growth. American Airlines might grow faster by taking market share, but that's a zero-sum game that leads to price wars.

The key question for a fast grower is: "How can growth continue?" For American Airlines, the answer is: it can't, not at a 20% rate. The company would need to double its fleet every three and a half years to sustain that growth, and that's not happening.

**2. Asset Play**

An asset play is when the assets of the company are worth more than the stock price. I love these. I made a fortune on them. Think of the company where the stock is $8, but a division is worth $4, and the real estate is worth $7, so you're getting the rest of the business for negative $3.

Is American Airlines an asset play? Let's look at the balance sheet. The company has about $40 billion in assets, including aircraft, routes, and gates. But it also has about $35 billion in debt. Net asset value is around $5 billion, or about $7.50 per share. The stock is trading at $14.98, which is double the net asset value.

That's not an asset play. An asset play is when you're buying assets for less than they're worth. Here, you're paying a premium.

The only way this becomes an asset play is if the assets are undervalued on the balance sheet. Are the planes worth more than book value? Maybe, in a strong market. But airline assets are highly depreciable and have limited alternative uses. A Boeing 737 is worth a lot less if nobody wants to fly.

**3. Turnaround**

A turnaround is when a company has been beaten down but is showing signs of recovery. The key question is: "Is the plan working?"

American Airlines went through a restructuring in the pandemic. They took on massive debt, renegotiated labor contracts, and streamlined operations. The stock went from $5 to $20 and back to $15. Is the turnaround working?

The evidence is mixed. Revenue is recovering, but costs are rising. Fuel prices are volatile. Labor costs are going up. The company is still carrying a huge debt load. Interest payments alone are eating up a significant chunk of operating income.

For a true turnaround, I want to see three things: (1) a new management team with a credible plan, (2) debt restructuring that reduces interest payments, and (3) improving operating margins. American Airlines has some of these, but not all. The debt is still high. The margins are still thin. And the plan is basically "fly planes and hope for the best."

I've seen a lot of turnarounds in my career. The best ones are in companies that have a clear path to profitability. Think of Chrysler in the 1980s, or Ford in the 2000s. They had new products, new management, and a plan to cut costs. American Airlines doesn't have that kind of clarity.

**4. Cyclical**

This is the category American Airlines fits best. It's a classic cyclical: earnings go up and down with the economy, fuel prices, and industry capacity.

The key to buying cyclicals is to buy when earnings are terrible and the P/E is high, and sell when earnings are great and the P/E is low. That's counterintuitive, but it's the only way to make money.

Right now, earnings are terrible (31 cents per share) and the P/E is high (48.3). That suggests we might be near the bottom of the cycle. But here's the problem: how do you know when the bottom is? You don't. You can only buy when the story is so bad that nobody wants to own the stock.

**5. Stalwart (10-12% growth)**

A stalwart is a company that grows at a steady 10-12% rate, like Procter & Gamble or Coca-Cola. They're safe, boring, and reliable.

American Airlines is not a stalwart. Its earnings are too volatile. In 2019, the company earned $4.50 per share. In 2020, it lost $15 per share. That's not steady growth. That's a roller coaster.

Stalwarts are for protection. You buy them when you want to sleep at night. American Airlines will not let you sleep at night.

I have specific sell rules for each category. Let me apply them to American Airlines as a cyclical.

**For a cyclical stock, the sell rules are:**

1. **Inventory is building up.** In the airline industry, inventory is capacity — the number of seats available. If airlines are adding capacity faster than demand, that's a sell signal. Look at the industry data. Are airlines ordering new planes? Are they adding routes? If yes, the cycle is turning down.

2. **Capital spending is rising.** Airlines are capital-intensive. They need to buy planes, build terminals, and invest in technology. When capital spending is rising, it means management is optimistic. That's usually a sign that the cycle is near its peak.

3. **The P/E is low.** This is the paradox of cyclicals. When the P/E is low, it means earnings are high, and the stock is about to fall. When the P/E is high, it means earnings are low, and the stock is about to rise. Right now, the P/E is 48.3, which is high. That's actually a buy signal for a cyclical, not a sell signal.

But here's the catch: the P/E is high because earnings are depressed. If earnings don't recover, the stock will fall. So you need to be right about the recovery.

4. **The story is too good.** When everyone is talking about how great the airline industry is, sell. When everyone is talking about how terrible it is, buy. Right now, the story is mixed. Some people are bullish on the recovery. Some people are bearish on the debt load. That's not a clear signal.
