**SECTION 1: THE SIX CATEGORIES—WHERE DOES AUR FIT?**

**1.1 Slow Grower (2-4% growth)**

Slow growers are companies like utilities or old-line industrials that grow at the rate of the economy. They pay dividends. You own them for income. Aurora doesn’t pay a dividend. They’re not growing at 2-4%. They’re growing revenue but losing money. So not a Slow Grower.

**1.2 Stalwart (10-12% growth)**

Stalwarts are companies like Coca-Cola or Procter & Gamble. They grow consistently, they have strong brands, and they pay dividends. Aurora has no brand recognition among consumers, no dividend, and no consistent earnings. Not a Stalwart.

**1.3 Fast Grower (20-25% growth)**

This is the category that most speculators would put Aurora in. Fast growers are small, aggressive companies that grow earnings at 20-25% per year. Think of the old SCI (Service Corporation International) story—they bought funeral homes and grew earnings like clockwork. Or La Quinta Motor Inns—they built hotels and grew units at 20% per year.

But here’s the key: Fast growers MUST have earnings. You can’t grow earnings from a negative base. If you’re losing $0.43 per share, and you grow revenue 50%, you’re still losing money. The earnings growth rate is undefined. So Aurora doesn’t qualify as a Fast Grower in my book.

**1.4 Cyclical**

Cyclicals are companies like Ford, General Motors, or Caterpillar. Their earnings go up and down with the economy. When the economy is strong, they make money. When it’s weak, they lose money. Aurora’s business—autonomous driving—is not cyclical in the traditional sense. It’s more of a technology adoption curve. But if the economy goes into recession, companies might delay buying autonomous trucks. So there’s some cyclicality. But not enough to classify it as a Cyclical.

**1.5 Turnaround**

Turnarounds are companies that have been beaten down and are coming back. Think of Chrysler in the early 1980s, or Ford in 2009. They had debt, losses, and a plan to restructure. Aurora is not a turnaround because they’ve never been profitable. They’re a startup. Turnarounds have a history of profitability that they’re trying to restore. Aurora doesn’t have that history.

**1.6 Asset Play**

Asset plays are companies where the assets are worth more than the stock price. For example, a company might have $8 per share in stock, but a division worth $4 and real estate worth $7. So you’re getting the rest for negative $3. That’s a bargain.
