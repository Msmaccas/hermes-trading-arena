## GATE ONE: CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

Its primary business is taking deposits and making loans—the oldest business model in the world. It earns net interest income on the spread between what it pays depositors and what it charges borrowers. It also earns fees from insurance premiums, credit card transactions, asset management, and other services.

Now, do I understand banking? I've owned banks for decades. I owned Wells Fargo for 25 years. I owned Bank of America. I currently own a significant stake in Bank of America, though I've been reducing it. I understand the basics: a bank is a leveraged bet on the economy. It borrows short (deposits) and lends long (loans). Its success depends on credit underwriting, cost control, and the health of the economy in which it operates.

But here's where it gets tricky. Bradesco operates in Brazil. And Brazil is not the United States. It has different inflation dynamics, different interest rate policies, different regulatory frameworks, different political risks, and different currency risks. I've never owned a Brazilian bank. So the question becomes: Is Bradesco simple enough that I can understand it from Omaha?

## GATE TWO: MOAT ANALYSIS

*"What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?"*

Let me apply my four moat types to Bradesco.

**Cost Advantage:** Does Bradesco produce or deliver banking services cheaper than competitors? Brazilian banks have historically had high operating costs relative to global peers. The banking sector has high overhead from branch networks, employee costs, and regulatory compliance. However, Bradesco has been investing heavily in digital banking, and its digital platform, Next, has been gaining traction. The cost-to-income ratio for Brazilian banks has been improving but remains above 40% for most players. I don't see a clear cost advantage here. It's not GEICO, which has the lowest expense ratio in auto insurance at about 12% versus the industry average of 23%. Bradesco is not the low-cost producer.

**Network Effect:** Does Bradesco become more valuable the more it's used? Banking has some network effects—more depositors mean more liquidity, which allows more lending, which attracts more borrowers. But this is weak compared to something like American Express's closed-loop network or Visa's payment system. Bradesco has a large customer base—about 70 million clients—but the value of that base doesn't increase exponentially with each new customer. It's linear.

**High Switching Costs:** This is where Bradesco has a real moat. Switching banks is painful. You have to change direct deposits, automatic payments, credit card numbers, and bill pay setups. In Brazil, where banking is highly concentrated and digital banking is less ubiquitous than in the U.S., switching costs are even higher. Bradesco has deep relationships with corporate clients, payroll processing, and government contracts. Once a company or individual is in the Bradesco ecosystem, leaving is a hassle. This is a genuine moat.

**Intangible Assets:** Brand is important in banking. Bradesco is one of the most recognized brands in Brazil. It has been around since 1943. The brand signals stability and trust. In a country with a history of inflation and economic volatility, trust in a bank is a valuable intangible. Bradesco also has a strong insurance business—it's one of the largest insurers in Brazil through its partnership with the BB Seguridade group. Insurance brands have pricing power.

Now, let me ask the moat question directly: What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?

Foreign banks have tried to enter Brazil. Citibank, HSBC, Santander—they've all been there. Most have struggled. Santander has done reasonably well, but Citibank sold its Brazilian retail operations to Itaú in 2016. HSBC sold its Brazilian operations to Bradesco in 2016. The barriers to entry are high: you need a branch network, regulatory approvals, local expertise, and customer trust. The big five Brazilian banks have a oligopolistic grip on the market.

But here's what worries me. Fintechs are disrupting banking globally. In Brazil, Nubank has grown to over 80 million customers—more than Bradesco. Nubank is digital-only, has lower costs, and is growing rapidly. It's eating into the incumbents' market share. Bradesco has responded with its own digital offerings, but the threat is real. The moat from switching costs is being eroded by the convenience and lower fees of digital-only banks.

I also worry about the regulatory environment. Brazilian banks are subject to high taxes and regulatory costs. The government has periodically pressured banks to lower interest rates and fees. Political interference is a risk.

**Verdict on Gate Two:** Partial pass. Bradesco has a moat from switching costs and brand, but it's being challenged by fintech disruption and regulatory risk. The moat is not as wide or deep as what I see in Coca-Cola, American Express, or Moody's. It's more like the moat of a regional bank in the U.S.—defensible but not impregnable.

## GATE THREE: OWNER EARNINGS & INTRINSIC VALUE

*"It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."*

Let me calculate owner earnings for Bradesco.

**Owner Earnings = Net Income + Depreciation/Amortization - Maintenance Capital Expenditures**

For the trailing twelve months, Bradesco reported net income of approximately R$ 20 billion (Brazilian reais). At the current exchange rate of about R$ 5.5 to the dollar, that's roughly $3.6 billion in net income. But let me be careful here—Brazilian accounting standards differ from U.S. GAAP, and I need to adjust for non-recurring items.

Depreciation and amortization for a bank are relatively small—maybe R$ 2-3 billion annually. Maintenance capex for a bank is also relatively low—mostly technology investments and branch maintenance. Let's say R$ 3-4 billion annually.

So owner earnings might be around R$ 19-20 billion, or roughly $3.5-3.6 billion. At a market cap of $37 billion, that's a price-to-owner-earnings ratio of about 10.3x. That's not screamingly cheap, but it's not expensive either.

Now let me look at the key ratios I care about:

**ROE:** Bradesco's return on equity has been around 15-18% historically. That's decent but not great. Compare that to Apple, which has ROE well above 100% because it uses minimal equity relative to its earnings. For a bank, 15% ROE is acceptable but not exceptional. Itaú, Bradesco's main competitor, has historically had higher ROE.

**Debt/Equity:** Banks are inherently leveraged. Bradesco's debt-to-equity ratio is about 3:1, which is typical for a bank. I don't love leverage, but banks require it. The key is whether the leverage is stable and the cost of debt is low. Bradesco's funding base is primarily deposits, which are stable and low-cost.

**Capex/Net Income:** For a bank, this ratio is usually below 20%. Bradesco's is around 15-20%. That's fine. Low capital intensity means more free cash flow flows to shareholders.

**Dividend Yield:** 4.73%. That's attractive, but I need to check the payout ratio. Bradesco typically pays out about 40-50% of net income as dividends. That's sustainable. The remaining earnings are retained to grow the business.

**Retained Earnings Test:** Has Bradesco created at least $1 of market value for every $1 of retained earnings? Over the past decade, Bradesco's book value has grown from about R$ 80 billion to R$ 140 billion, while market cap has grown from about R$ 150 billion to R$ 200 billion (in reais). That's roughly $1.25 of market value per dollar of retained earnings. Not great, but not terrible. Compare that to Berkshire, where each dollar retained has created about $3-4 of market value over time.

Now let me estimate intrinsic value.

I'll use a simple discounted cash flow model. If Bradesco can grow owner earnings at 5% annually (roughly in line with Brazilian nominal GDP growth) for the next 10 years, and I discount at the long-term U.S. Treasury rate of about 4.5%, the present value of those earnings is roughly $45-50 billion. Add in terminal value, and I get an intrinsic value of $50-60 billion, or about $4.50-5.50 per share.

At $3.50, the stock is trading at about 60-70% of my conservative intrinsic estimate. That's within my buy range, but barely.

But here's the problem: I'm using U.S. Treasury rates to discount Brazilian real earnings. That's not quite right. I should use Brazilian real interest rates, which are much higher—the Brazilian 10-year bond yield is around 12%. If I discount at 12%, the intrinsic value drops to $25-30 billion, or about $2.50-3.00 per share.

So the stock looks cheap in dollar terms but expensive in real terms. This is the currency trap. The Brazilian real has depreciated about 5-7% annually against the dollar over the past decade. If that continues, dollar-based investors will see their returns eroded.

**Verdict on Gate Three:** Uncertain pass. The owner earnings are real, but the currency risk makes intrinsic value estimation highly uncertain. I'd need a much wider margin of safety to compensate for that uncertainty.
