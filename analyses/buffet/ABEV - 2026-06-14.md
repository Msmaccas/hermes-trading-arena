## GATE TWO: MOAT ANALYSIS — WHAT KEEPS COMPETITORS OUT FOR TEN YEARS?

Let me apply the four types of moat I look for.

**Cost Advantage:** Ambev has one of the lowest cost structures in the global brewing industry. They have scale. They have vertical integration. They own their own distribution networks in Brazil, which is critical because Brazil is a continental-sized country with challenging logistics. The cost to replicate their distribution system would be billions of dollars and take years. Their breweries are modern and efficient. Their operating margins have historically been among the highest in the industry, often exceeding 40% in their core Brazilian operations. That is a real cost advantage.

**Network Effect:** This is not a classic network effect business like American Express or Moody's. The value of a beer does not increase because more people drink it. However, there is a distribution network effect. The more products you distribute through your system, the more efficient your system becomes. Ambev distributes their own beers, but they also distribute for third parties, including PepsiCo products in Brazil. That gives them density and lowers their per-unit cost. That is a form of network effect, though not as powerful as a payment network.

**High Switching Costs:** For the end consumer, switching costs are low. If you prefer Brahma or Skol, you can buy them anywhere. But for the retailer, switching costs are high. Ambev controls shelf space in thousands of bars, restaurants, and grocery stores across Brazil. They have sales teams that visit accounts multiple times per week. They provide coolers, signage, and point-of-sale materials. If a retailer wants to replace Ambev's products, they have to rip out the infrastructure and retrain their staff. That is a real switching cost.

**Intangible Assets:** The brand portfolio is strong. Brahma, Skol, Antarctica, and Guaraná Antarctica are household names in Brazil. These brands have been around for generations. They have pricing power. Ambev has demonstrated the ability to raise prices ahead of inflation in many years. That is the hallmark of a strong brand. However, I must note that brand loyalty in beer is not as strong as it is in soft drinks. People will switch to a cheaper option if the price gap is wide enough. But within a reasonable range, the brands hold.

**The Moat Question:** What keeps competitors out for ten years? The answer is distribution, scale, and cost structure. A new entrant would need to build a national distribution network in Brazil, which is one of the most complex markets in the world. They would need to invest billions in breweries. They would need to build brand awareness from zero. And they would face a competitor with 40%+ operating margins who can afford to fight a price war. That is a wide moat.

But I must also consider the threats. The biggest threat is not competition from other brewers. It is government regulation, particularly in Brazil. The Brazilian government has a history of interfering in the beer industry through tax policy, price controls, and antitrust enforcement. Ambev has been fined multiple times for anticompetitive behavior. The regulatory risk is real and cannot be ignored.
