### GATE ONE: THE CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

**The Test:** Can I explain in five minutes how this business makes money, who its customers are, and why they stay? If not, I do not invest. Period.

I can answer this one in about thirty seconds. Ambev makes and sells beer. They are the dominant brewer in Latin America, specifically in Brazil, which is their home market. They also sell soft drinks, non-alcoholic beverages, and other malt-based products. They own brands like Skol, Brahma, Antarctica, and Budweiser (under license for most of Latin America).

Their customers are thirsty people. Their customers are bars, restaurants, and supermarkets. Their customers are you and me, after a long day of work, looking for a cold beverage. The business model is simple: They brew beer, put it in a can or a bottle, ship it to a distributor, and sell it for more than it costs to make.

Why do customers stay? Because of brand habit. In Brazil, if you grew up drinking Skol or Brahma, you don't switch to a generic brand just because it's a nickel cheaper. There is a tribal loyalty to beer brands. It's a cultural product. I understand this. I understood it when I bought Coca-Cola in 1988. I understood it when I bought See's Candy in 1972. People like what they like. They don't switch easily.

Furthermore, the distribution network is a fortress. Ambev has the most extensive direct distribution system in Brazil. They own their own trucks, their own warehouses, and their own sales force. They can get a cold beer to a remote village in the Amazon faster than any competitor can. This is a massive structural advantage. If you are a bar owner in São Paulo, you don't call three different distributors. You call Ambev. They show up every Tuesday and Thursday. You trust them.

**Verdict on Gate One:** Pass. This is a simple, understandable business. It is within my circle of competence. I know how they make money. I know who their customers are. I know why they stay.

### GATE TWO: THE MOAT ANALYSIS

**The Test:** What keeps competitors out for 10+ years? If you can't answer, do NOT invest.

This is where the analysis gets interesting. Ambev has a moat, but it is not as wide as Coca-Cola's, and it is not as deep as Apple's. Let's look at the four types of moats.

**1. Cost Advantage:**
This is Ambev's strongest weapon. They are the low-cost producer in Brazil. The industry is dominated by a duopoly: Ambev and Heineken (which owns Kirin and other brands in Brazil). Ambev has roughly 60-65% market share. Heineken has about 25-30%. The rest are tiny craft players.

Because of their scale, Ambev can negotiate better prices on raw materials (barley, hops, aluminum for cans). Because of their distribution network, they have the lowest logistics cost per unit. The industry average expense ratio for selling, general, and administrative costs (SG&A) is probably around 35-40% of revenue for a smaller player. Ambev runs at about 30-32%. That 5-10 percentage point advantage is pure profit over time. It means they can price their beer lower than Heineken and still make a better return on capital. Or, they can price the same and make a fortune.

**2. High Switching Costs:**
This is weaker for beer than it is for, say, Moody's (where switching rating agencies is a regulatory nightmare). If a bar owner is unhappy with Ambev's service, they *could* switch to Heineken. But it's painful. The bar owner has to change their ordering system, change their cooler placement, change their promotional schedule. Ambev makes it easy. They provide the coolers for free. They provide the point-of-sale materials. They provide the sales rep who knows your name. The switching cost is not technical, it is *logistical and relational*. It is a "pain in the neck" moat. It is real, but it is not permanent.

**3. Intangible Assets (Brand):**
This is a real moat, but it is regional. The brand power of Skol and Brahma in Brazil is enormous. It is not Coca-Cola globally, but in Brazil, it is close. A Brazilian consumer will pay a premium for a Skol over a generic beer because it signifies quality, consistency, and social status. This is pricing power. During the last recession in Brazil (2015-2016), Ambev was able to raise prices and only lost a small amount of volume. They have pricing power.

**4. Network Effect:**
Beer does not have a traditional network effect. The beer does not get better because more people drink it. But the *distribution* network has a quasi-network effect. The more customers Ambev serves, the more routes they run, the lower the cost per delivery, and the better the service. This creates a barrier to entry for new competitors who cannot afford to build a national distribution network from scratch.

**The Moat Question:** What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?
The answer is **Scale + Distribution + Brand.** A new competitor would need to spend $5 billion to $10 billion to build a brewery and a distribution network to compete with Ambev in Brazil. They would then need to spend another $1 billion on marketing to convince Brazilians to switch from Skol. It is possible, but it is incredibly difficult. The moat is "wide enough."

**Verdict on Gate Two:** Pass. The moat is not impenetrable, but it is durable. Cost advantage and brand power are the primary barriers.

**Calculating Owner Earnings:**

Let's look at Ambev's historical financials. Over the last 5 years (2021-2025), Ambev has generated roughly the following annual averages:
- Net Income: ~$3.5 Billion USD (at current exchange rates)
- Depreciation & Amortization: ~$800 Million
- Capital Expenditures: ~$700 Million

Now, the critical question: **Maintenance Capex vs. Growth Capex.**
Ambev is a capital-intensive business in terms of distribution. They need to buy trucks, build warehouses, and maintain breweries. However, they have been in business for decades. Most of their breweries are already built. Most of their distribution network is already in place.

Looking at their financial statements, they report total Capex of about $700M. I estimate that **Maintenance Capex** (what they need to spend just to keep the current business running) is about **$500M**. The other $200M is for growth (new markets, new products).

**Owner Earnings Formula:**
`Owner Earnings = Net Income + Depreciation - Maintenance Capex`

`Owner Earnings = $3.5B + $0.8B - $0.5B = $3.8 Billion`

So, the business is generating roughly **$3.8 Billion** in true, distributable owner earnings per year.

**What about the Dividend?**
The current dividend yield is 4.76%. At a $50.4B market cap, that implies a total dividend payout of about **$2.4 Billion**.

This means Ambev is paying out about 63% of its owner earnings as dividends. The rest ($1.4 Billion) is being retained by the business. This is a good sign. It means management is not over-distributing. They are keeping capital to reinvest.

**The Retained Earnings Test:**
This is a test I invented years ago. Every dollar retained by the business must create at least one dollar of market value over time. Ambev has a Return on Equity (ROE) of about 18-20%. This is excellent. It means for every $100 they keep in the business, they earn $18-20 per year. If they can reinvest that $1.4 Billion at 18% returns, then the retained earnings are working for the shareholder. This passes the test.

Currently, the stock trades at 17.1x earnings. That is a 5.8% earnings yield. That is not a screaming bargain.
