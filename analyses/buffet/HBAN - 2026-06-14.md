## GATE TWO: MOAT ANALYSIS

**The test:** What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?

Huntington’s moat is modest. It is not a wide moat like Coca-Cola or Moody’s. It is not a deep moat like American Express. But it has a moat. Let me explain.

**First, local deposit franchise.** Huntington has a strong branch network in the Midwest. It has 1,100 branches. It has a 9% deposit market share in Ohio. It has a 6% share in Michigan. These are sticky deposits. People do not move their checking accounts for a few basis points. They move for convenience, for relationship, for trust. Huntington has been in these communities for 150 years. That is a moat. It is not a fortress, but it is a moat.

**Second, cost of deposits.** This is the single most important metric for a bank. If you have low-cost deposits, you can lend at lower rates and still make a profit. If you have high-cost deposits, you are at the mercy of the market. Huntington’s cost of deposits is around 1.5% to 2.0% as of early 2026. That is not the best in the industry—JPMorgan and Bank of America have costs below 1%—but it is better than many of its regional peers. It is a moat, but a thin one.

**Third, relationship banking.** Huntington focuses on small and medium-sized businesses (SMEs) and consumer banking. They have a program called "The Huntington National Bank" that offers free checking for students, free checking for seniors, and a "24-Hour Grace" overdraft protection program. These are not revolutionary, but they create switching costs. If you have your business checking, your payroll, your credit card processing, and your mortgage all with one bank, you are not leaving for a 0.25% lower rate on a CD. That is a moat.

**Fourth, scale.** Huntington has $200 billion in assets. That is large enough to have a cost advantage over community banks, but small enough to be nimble compared to the megabanks. They can invest in technology—they have a decent mobile app—but they do not have the scale of JPMorgan. That is a mixed bag.

**The moat question:** Can a competitor replicate Huntington’s business in the Midwest? The answer is: yes, but it would take a long time and a lot of money. You would need to build 1,100 branches, hire 20,000 employees, build a brand, and earn the trust of customers. That is not impossible, but it is hard. It is a moat, but it is not a wide one.

**Verdict:** Pass. Modest moat. Not a fortress, but defensible.

That is the cash the business generates that can be returned to shareholders or reinvested. It is not a perfect number—banks have to retain capital for growth and regulatory requirements—but it is a good approximation.

### Intrinsic Value

I use the long-term US Treasury yield as my discount rate. As of June 2026, the 10-year Treasury is around 4.2%. I do not use WACC. I do not use CAPM. I use the risk-free rate because I am comparing the business to a bond.

If owner earnings are $2.55 billion, and I assume they can grow at 3% to 5% per year (in line with nominal GDP growth), the intrinsic value of the business is:

Value = Owner Earnings / (Discount Rate - Growth Rate)

Using a discount rate of 4.2% and a growth rate of 3%, the value is: $2.55B / (0.042 - 0.03) = $2.55B / 0.012 = **$212.5 billion**.

That is too high. That implies the stock is worth $105 per share. That is nonsense. The market is not that stupid. The reason is that banks cannot grow owner earnings at 3% without retaining a lot of capital. The growth is not free. You have to reinvest a significant portion of earnings to maintain the capital ratios.

So let me use a more conservative approach. I will assume that owner earnings grow at 2% per year, and I will use a discount rate of 5% to reflect the risk and the capital requirements. That gives:

Value = $2.55B / (0.05 - 0.02) = $2.55B / 0.03 = **$85 billion**.

That is $42 per share. Still too high. The market is pricing it at $17.52. Why? Because the market is discounting a lot of bad news: recession, credit losses, regulatory changes, or a secular decline in banking.

Let me use a third approach. I will look at tangible book value. HBAN has tangible book value per share of approximately $12.50. Historically, regional banks trade at 1.5x to 2.0x tangible book.
