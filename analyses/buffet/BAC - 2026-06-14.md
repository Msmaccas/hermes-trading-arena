## Gate One: The Circle of Competence

The first question I ask is: Can I explain how this business makes money, who its customers are, and why they stay, in five minutes? If I can't, I walk away.

Bank of America is within my circle, but it's not a simple circle. It's not See's Candy. It's not GEICO. It's a massive, interconnected financial machine. Here's my five-minute explanation:

Bank of America makes money in three primary ways. First, and most importantly, it takes in deposits—cheap, sticky, federally insured money—and lends it out at a higher rate. This is the classic spread business. The difference between what they pay on deposits and what they earn on loans (the net interest margin) is their bread and butter. Second, they earn fees. They manage assets for wealthy clients (Merrill Lynch). They process credit card transactions. They underwrite bonds and stocks for corporations (BofA Securities). They charge fees for checking accounts and overdrafts. Third, they trade. They have a massive trading desk that deals in everything from Treasury bonds to derivatives.

The customers are everyone. The individual who gets a mortgage in Charlotte. The small business owner in San Francisco who uses their merchant services. The Fortune 500 CFO who uses their investment bank to issue a bond. The retiree who has their IRA with Merrill Edge. This is a universal bank.

Why do they stay? For individuals, it's inertia and convenience. Moving your checking account is a pain in the neck. For corporations, it's the depth of the relationship. BofA can lend them $1 billion, help them issue stock, and provide treasury management services. The switching cost is enormous. For Merrill Lynch clients, it's the personal relationship with a financial advisor. This creates a very wide, but not unassailable, moat.

However, there's a crucial point. The banking business is inherently leveraged. You take in $10 of deposits, lend out $9, and keep $1 of equity. That leverage amplifies gains in good times and can destroy you in bad times. The moat can be broken by a run on the bank, a catastrophic loan book, or a regulatory change. I understand this. I lived through the 2008 financial crisis. I saw the pain. I saw the opportunity. So yes, this is within my circle. But it requires constant vigilance. It is not a business you can set and forget for 50 years without worry. It requires a rational, risk-averse management team at the helm. We'll get to that.

## Gate Two: The Moat Analysis

What keeps competitors out for 10+ years? Let's apply the four types of moats.

**Cost Advantage:** This is not BofA's primary moat. They are not the low-cost producer. They have a massive branch network, a huge workforce, and a legacy technology stack. Their efficiency ratio (expenses as a % of revenue) is around 60-65%. Compare that to a pure online bank like Ally, which has a much lower cost structure. BofA's cost advantage, if it exists, comes from its massive scale. They can spread their fixed costs (technology, compliance, risk management) over a huge base of assets. But they are not GEICO, which has a structural cost advantage in the insurance industry. This is a medium moat at best.

**Network Effect:** This is a very real moat for BofA. Consider the "closed-loop" nature of their financial ecosystem. A corporation uses BofA's treasury management services. Their employees get paid via direct deposit into BofA accounts. Those employees use BofA credit cards. The corporation issues debt through BofA's investment bank. The more people and businesses that use BofA, the more valuable the network becomes for everyone. This is similar, in a small way, to the network effect American Express had. As I said about Amex, "they were getting a premium for American Express tickets... they can sell their traveler's check for more than Citigroup, Bank of America, Barclays." BofA doesn't have that exact pricing power, but the stickiness of the ecosystem is real. This is a strong moat.

**High Switching Costs:** This is BofA's single strongest moat. For a corporate client, switching banks is a nightmare. It takes months, costs millions, and risks disrupting their entire treasury operations. They have to reprogram payment systems, renegotiate credit lines, and move payroll. They are not going to do that for a few basis points on a loan. For an individual, it's easier but still a hassle. You have to change direct deposit, update automatic bill payments, and order new checks. Most people simply don't bother. As I've said, the best business is one that "has a moat around it that protects it from competition." For BofA, the moat is the sheer friction of leaving. This is a very wide moat.

**Intangible Assets (Brand & Pricing Power):** This is a mixed bag. The Bank of America brand is not like Coca-Cola. It's not a beloved brand. It's a utility. People don't *want* to have a relationship with their bank; they *need* to. However, the brand does have trust, which is the most important intangible in banking. After 2008, trust was shattered. BofA spent a decade rebuilding it. That trust is a valuable asset, but it is fragile. They have some pricing power on fees and credit card rates, but they are constrained by competition and regulation. They cannot raise mortgage rates willy-nilly. Their pricing power is real but limited. This is a moderate moat.

**Overall Moat Assessment:** Bank of America has a wide, but not impenetrable, moat. It is built on high switching costs and a network effect. It is *not* a moat like See's Candy or Coca-Cola, which can raise prices every year without losing a customer. It is a moat like a regulated utility, but with more risk. The moat can be breached by a massive financial crisis, a major technological disruption (like a successful fintech that cracks the corporate banking code), or a catastrophic management failure. The moat is real, but it requires constant maintenance. It is not a "wonderful business" in the purest sense, but it is a *good* business with a durable competitive position.

## Gate Three: Owner Earnings & Intrinsic Value

This is where the rubber meets the road. What is this business actually worth, and what is it earning for its owners?

Let's look at the raw numbers. The P/E is 13.9. The EPS is $4.03. The earnings growth is 16.6% quarterly. Those are the GAAP numbers. But I don't trust GAAP earnings for a bank. They are full of assumptions about loan loss reserves, deferred tax assets, and the fair value of complex securities. I want to get to Owner Earnings.

**The Owner Earnings Formula:** `Owner Earnings = Net Income + Depreciation/Amortization - Maintenance Capital Expenditures`

For a bank, "Maintenance CapEx" is a tricky concept. They don't have factories. Their capital expenditures are on technology, branches, and data centers. A significant portion of that is maintenance—keeping the lights on, upgrading the website, complying with new regulations. But some of it is growth—opening new branches in Texas, building a new trading platform.

Let me be generous and assume that 50% of their total CapEx is maintenance. In 2025, BofA spent roughly $4 billion on CapEx. So maintenance is $2 billion. Their net income was approximately $30 billion. Depreciation and amortization is roughly $4 billion.

So, Owner Earnings = $30B + $4B - $2B = **$32 billion**.

That is the real cash flow the business generated last year, after keeping the business in its current competitive position. That's a 32/397.55 = 8% Owner Earnings Yield. That's not bad.

**Key Thresholds:**
- **Free Cash Flow Conversion:** This is where banks get tricky. They generate massive "cash" from net income, but they are required by regulators to hold a lot of that cash as capital. They can't just send it all to shareholders. They have to maintain a Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio. The true "free cash flow" to shareholders is what they can pay out in dividends and buybacks *after* meeting regulatory capital requirements. In 2025, BofA returned about $20 billion to shareholders via dividends and buybacks. That's a 5% yield on the current market cap. That's a reasonable, but not spectacular, conversion rate.
- **Capex/Net Income Ratio:** $4B CapEx / $30B Net Income = 13%. This is excellent. It shows the business is not capital-intensive in the traditional sense. They don't need to spend a fortune to maintain their earnings power. This is a good sign.
- **ROE:** Bank of America's Return on Tangible Common Equity (ROTCE) is around 16-18%. This is a solid number. It means they are earning a good return on the capital their owners have put into the business. However, remember that this return is amplified by leverage. If they had less leverage, the ROE would be lower. But for a well-run bank, 16-18% is a good number.
- **Debt/Equity:** This is the critical number. BofA's debt-to-equity ratio is around 2.5. That's high, but it's normal for a bank. The key is the *quality* of that debt. Most of it is deposits, which are sticky and low-cost. They also have long-term debt. The risk is that in a crisis, the debt markets freeze, and they can't roll over their short-term borrowings. This is why liquidity is king. As I said, "We will always have cash around and we'll have treasury bills. We won't have money market funds. We didn't have them in 2008." BofA has a massive liquidity buffer. But the leverage is a risk I cannot ignore.

**Intrinsic Value Calculation:**

I use the long-term US Treasury yield as my discount rate. Let's say that's 4.5% today.

If I assume BofA can grow its Owner Earnings by 3% per year (in line with nominal GDP growth) for the next 10 years, and then 2% in perpetuity, the intrinsic value of the entire company is roughly:

- Year 1 Owner Earnings: $32B
- Discount Rate: 4.5%
- Terminal Growth Rate: 2%

Using a simple discounted cash flow model (which I do in my head, not on a spreadsheet), the intrinsic value is approximately **$550 - $650 billion**. That implies a per-share value of roughly **$75 - $90**.

At $56.02, the stock is selling at a significant discount to my conservative estimate of intrinsic value. It is not a screaming bargain like I saw in 2008 or 2020, but it is a fair price for a wonderful business.

**The Retained Earnings Test:** This is the most important test for a bank. Every dollar of earnings they retain (instead of paying out as a dividend) should create more than one dollar of market value. Over the last 10 years, BofA has retained roughly $150 billion in earnings. The market value of the company has increased by roughly $200 billion. That's a ratio of 1.33:1. That passes the test, but barely. It shows they are not destroying value, but they are not creating it at an extraordinary rate either.

**The Buyback Test:** BofA has been a big buyer of its own stock. In 2025, they bought back roughly $15 billion worth. If they are buying back stock below intrinsic value (which they are, at $56 vs. my $75-90 estimate), that is a huge green flag. It means management is acting as rational capital allocators. They are using the company's excess cash to retire shares at a discount, increasing the ownership stake of the remaining partners. This is exactly what I like to see.
