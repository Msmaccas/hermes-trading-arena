## Gate One: The Circle of Competence — Do I Understand This Business?

The first question I always ask is: Can I explain how this business makes money, who its customers are, and why they stay, in five minutes? For Microsoft, the answer is a qualified "yes," but it requires a caveat. I don’t understand the technology. I don’t understand the code. I don’t understand the architecture of Azure. But I *do* understand a customer who is locked in.

Microsoft’s business is built on two pillars: **Office 365 (now Microsoft 365)** and **Azure**. Let’s start with the first. Microsoft 365 is a subscription product. You pay a monthly fee, and you get Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, Teams, and a terabyte of cloud storage. Once you are in that ecosystem, leaving is painful. You have to migrate your emails, your documents, your workflows. As I said about American Express in 1964, "They were getting a premium... because the customer was locked in." The same is true for Microsoft 365. It’s a toll bridge that every enterprise, every school, and every government agency must cross.

The second pillar is Azure. This is more complex. Azure is a cloud computing platform that competes with Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Google Cloud. It’s a capital-intensive business. You have to build data centers, buy servers, and hire engineers. But once a customer chooses Azure, they often build their entire IT infrastructure on it. They use Azure Active Directory for identity management, Azure DevOps for software development, and Azure Machine Learning for AI. The switching costs are enormous. As I’ve said about Berkshire’s railroad, BNSF, "It’s a business that will be around 50 or 100 years from now." The same is true for Azure. The demand for cloud computing is not going away. It’s growing.

But here’s the caveat: I don’t understand the *pace of change*. In the technology world, moats can erode faster than you think. Five years ago, everyone was talking about Google Cloud as a distant third. Today, it’s gaining share. Five years ago, everyone thought on-premise servers were dead. Today, some companies are bringing workloads back. I don’t have the technical expertise to judge whether Microsoft’s AI investments in OpenAI will pay off or whether they will be disrupted by a new entrant. As I’ve said, "I don’t invest in what I do not understand." I understand the *business model* of Microsoft, but I don’t understand the *technology* well enough to predict its future with confidence. That’s a yellow flag.

**Verdict:** Passes the gate, but barely. I can explain the moat, but I can’t judge the pace of its erosion.

## Gate Two: The Moat — What Keeps Competitors Out for 10+ Years?

Now let’s talk about the moat. Microsoft has three of the four types I look for.

**1. High Switching Costs (The Strongest Moat):**
This is Microsoft’s superpower. Think about a large enterprise like General Electric or Procter & Gamble. They have thousands of employees using Microsoft 365. They have millions of documents stored in SharePoint. They have custom applications built on Azure. To switch to Google Workspace or a different cloud provider, they would have to retrain their entire workforce, migrate petabytes of data, and rebuild their applications. The cost and disruption are enormous. As I’ve said about Moody’s, "It’s a business that would be very painful to leave." The same is true for Microsoft.

**2. Intangible Assets (Brand and Patents):**
Microsoft has a powerful brand. It’s trusted by IT departments around the world. It also has a massive patent portfolio. But let me be honest: brand alone is not enough. Coca-Cola has a brand, but people can drink Pepsi. Microsoft’s brand is a moat only because it’s combined with switching costs. The brand reinforces the lock-in.

**3. Network Effects (Partial):**
This is where Microsoft is weaker than some think. Office 365 has some network effects: if your colleagues use Teams, you have to use Teams. But it’s not a true two-sided network like Visa or American Express. Azure has some network effects: the more developers use Azure, the more tools and libraries are built for it. But it’s not as strong as the network effects of Facebook or Google Search.

**4. Cost Advantage (Weak):**
Microsoft is not a low-cost producer. Azure is actually more expensive than AWS for some workloads. Microsoft competes on features and lock-in, not price.

**The Moat Question:** "What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?" The answer is the combination of switching costs and the ecosystem. But I worry about the *erosion* of that moat. Five years ago, Microsoft’s moat was impenetrable. Today, I see cracks. Google is winning in AI. Amazon is winning in price. And the open-source movement is chipping away at the edges. As I’ve said, "The most important thing to look for is a moat that is widening." Is Microsoft’s moat widening? I’m not sure. It might be staying the same, or it might be narrowing slightly.

**Verdict:** Passes the gate, but with reservations. The moat is wide, but I’m not convinced it’s getting wider.

## Gate Three: Owner Earnings & Intrinsic Value — The Arithmetic of $2.9 Trillion

Now we get to the heart of the matter. This is where the math gets brutal. Let me run the numbers.

**Owner Earnings Formula:**
`Owner Earnings = Net Income + Depreciation/Amortization - Maintenance Capital Expenditures`

For fiscal year 2025 (ended June 2025), Microsoft reported:
- Net Income: $95.5 billion
- Depreciation & Amortization: $28.4 billion
- Capital Expenditures (total): $55.6 billion

But wait. Not all capital expenditures are "maintenance." Microsoft is spending billions on new data centers for Azure and AI. Some of that is growth capex, not maintenance. Let me be conservative and assume that 50% of capex is maintenance. That gives us:
- Maintenance Capex: $27.8 billion
- Owner Earnings: $95.5B + $28.4B - $27.8B = **$96.1 billion**

So, Microsoft generates roughly $96 billion in owner earnings per year. That’s a lot. But the market is valuing the company at $2.9 trillion. That gives us a owner earnings yield of:
- $96B / $2,902B = **3.3%**

Let me put that in perspective. A 10-year U.S. Treasury bond currently yields about 4.5%. So, Microsoft’s owner earnings yield is lower than a risk-free government bond. That means you are paying a premium for the "growth" and "moat" of Microsoft. And that premium is enormous.

**Intrinsic Value Calculation:**
Let me use a simple discounted cash flow model. Assume Microsoft can grow its owner earnings at 10% per year for the next 10 years (a very optimistic assumption), and then at 3% forever (the long-term GDP growth rate). Using a 4.5% discount rate (the 10-year Treasury yield), the intrinsic value comes to:
- Present Value of 10-year cash flows: ~$1.2 trillion
- Terminal Value: ~$3.5 trillion
- Total Intrinsic Value: ~$4.7 trillion

That sounds like a lot. But remember, this assumes 10% growth for a decade. That’s a heroic assumption. If growth slows to 5% (which is more realistic given Microsoft’s size), the intrinsic value drops to:
- Total Intrinsic Value: ~$3.1 trillion

At $2.9 trillion, Microsoft is trading at a 6% discount to my optimistic case and a 6% premium to my realistic case. That’s not a margin of safety. That’s a coin flip.

**Key Ratios:**
- **ROE:** 38% (excellent)
- **Debt/Equity:** 0.3 (low, but not zero)
- **Capex/Net Income:** 58% (high, indicating capital intensity)
- **Free Cash Flow Conversion:** 67% (decent, but not great)
- **Insider Ownership:** Satya Nadella owns about 0.01% of the company (meaningful, but not huge)

**The Retained Earnings Test:**
Every dollar Microsoft retains should create at least one dollar of market value. Over the past 10 years, Microsoft has retained about $200 billion in earnings. The market value has increased by about $2 trillion. That’s a 10x return. That’s outstanding. But the question is: can they do it again? The law of large numbers suggests it will be harder.

**On Apple’s Economics (for comparison):**
As I said in my 2026 interview, "Apple is a business that you've got one, probably and your kids have got them... I think it's more predictable in a certain sense, that the railroad will be around 50 or 100 years from now, but it doesn't earn the rate remotely on capital than Apple does." Apple’s ROE is over 100% because it’s asset-light. Microsoft’s ROE is 38% because it has to spend billions on data centers. That’s a meaningful difference.

**Verdict:** Fails the gate. The price is too high relative to the intrinsic value. There is no margin of safety.

## Gate Four: The Five-Year Test — Would I Be Happy If the Market Closed?

Here’s the question I always ask myself: "If the stock market closed for five years tomorrow, would I be happy owning this business based on its cash flow alone?"

For Microsoft, the answer is: **Yes, but only at a lower price.**

Let me explain. Microsoft generates $96 billion in owner earnings per year. Over five years, that’s $480 billion in cumulative owner earnings. The company also has $75 billion in net cash on its balance sheet. So, the total value you would get over five years is about $555 billion. But you are paying $2.9 trillion today. That means you are paying $2.9 trillion for a business that will return $555 billion in cash over five years. That’s a 19% return on your investment over five years, or about 3.5% annualized. That’s less than a Treasury bond.

Now, you might say, "But Warren, the business will be worth more in five years!" And that’s true. If Microsoft grows at 10% per year, the owner earnings will be $155 billion in year five. The terminal value might be $4 trillion. But that’s speculation. That’s betting on growth. As I’ve said, "The stock market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient." If you are buying Microsoft today, you are betting that the growth will continue at a blistering pace. If it doesn’t, you will lose money.

**The Casino Analogy:**
I’ve said many times, "You have this incredible cathedral called the American Economic System. Nobody's seen anything like it. I mean, it's the cathedral of all cathedrals. But attached to it is a casino and people can walk back and forth between the two. And believe me, people like to gamble." Microsoft is a great business, but at $390, it’s a casino chip. People are buying it because they think the price will go up, not because they think the business is undervalued.

**Verdict:** Fails the gate. I would not be happy owning Microsoft at this price if the market closed for five years.
