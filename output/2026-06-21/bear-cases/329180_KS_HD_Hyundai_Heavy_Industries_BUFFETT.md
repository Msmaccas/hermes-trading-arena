# HD Hyundai Heavy Industries — A Shipbuilder Priced Like a Tech Blue Chip

**Verdict: AVOID at any price above ₩380,000**

---

## One-Paragraph Business Summary

HD Hyundai Heavy Industries builds ships — big ones. LNG carriers, container ships, naval vessels. It operates three shipyards in South Korea and is trying to sell one of them (Gunsan) because they found out, the hard way, what happens when a cyclical downturn hits a capital-intensive business. It's a simple business to understand: they take orders, build ships over 2-3 years, deliver them, and pray steel prices and exchange rates don't eat the profit margin during the build cycle. You can explain this to a fifth grader: "They build things that go in the ocean, and sometimes business is great, and sometimes it's not."

## Circle of Competence — PASS

I can understand shipbuilding. It's not a technology company with 17 products I've never heard of. You take steel, you bend it, you weld it, you make a ship. I understand the business. The question isn't whether I understand it — the question is whether anyone should want to own it.

## Moat Analysis — NONE

This is where the rubber meets the road, and the rubber has a puncture. Let me walk through the four types of moats:

**Cost Advantage?** Korean shipbuilders have lower costs than European yards, but so do Chinese yards — and I'm told Chinese yards are building ships cheaper every year. That's not a moat, that's a treadmill you have to keep running on.

**Network Effect?** There is no network effect in shipbuilding. One customer buying a ship doesn't make the next customer's ship more valuable. It's the opposite — when one customer buys a ship, it adds to global shipping capacity and makes your next potential customer slightly less likely to need one.

**High Switching Costs?** The customer who buys an LNG carrier today could buy one from a Chinese yard next year. Switching costs in shipbuilding are close to zero. "If you're in a business where a competitor can take your customer by offering a slightly lower price, you have no moat." That's exactly shipbuilding.

**Intangible Assets?** There are some. Hyundai has a reputation for quality LNG carriers. But patents don't protect shipbuilding designs for long, and the brand premium is thin — especially when your customer is a state-owned energy company buying in bulk based on price and delivery terms.

My assessment is clear: **No Moat**. And in my experience, businesses without moats are not wonderful businesses. They're commodity businesses fighting on price and contract terms, and the only thing that protects them is when the cycle is favorable — which is exactly when people pay 19.7x forward earnings for them.

## Owner Earnings Calculation

This is where shipbuilding gets ugly. I've written for years about how GAAP earnings can lie, particularly for capital-intensive businesses. "The question is not what the company *reported*, but what it can *keep*."

Let me run the numbers:

- Net Income (latest TTM): approximately ₩2.6 trillion
- Depreciation & Amortization: approximately ₩400 billion
- Maintenance CapEx: This is the hard part. A shipyard requires constant investment — dry docks, gantry cranes, welding equipment, facilities. I conservatively estimate maintenance CapEx at 60-70% of reported CapEx, or approximately ₩500-600 billion annually.

Owner Earnings ≈ ₩2.6T + ₩0.4T - ₩0.6T = **₩2.4 trillion**

That's actually not terrible. But here's the problem — this figure assumes the cycle stays where it is. When the LNG carrier market collapsed 82.9% in H1 2026 and Korean yards hit only 50-70% of their annual order targets, what do you think happens to net income in 2-3 years when those orders are supposed to be delivering?

The answer: it collapses. And ₩2.4 trillion in owner earnings becomes maybe ₩800 billion — or less.

## The Five-Year Test

"If the stock market closed for five years tomorrow, would I be happy owning this business based on its cash flow alone?"

I ask myself this about every business I look at. For HD Hyundai Heavy Industries, the answer is an emphatic **NO**.

Here's why: in five years, we will almost certainly be in a different part of the shipbuilding cycle. The global LNG carrier order book is 82.9% lower than last year. Korean yards have $23.6 billion in Qatar orders to deliver — massive execution risk on complex vessels where delays and cost overruns are the norm, not the exception.

In the last down cycle, Hyundai's own Gunsan shipyard — completed in 2010 — had operations halted in 2017. A brand new facility, idled. That's what happens in shipbuilding. Now they're trying to sell it. That's not a sign of a business that compounds value through cycles. That's a business that inflates during upcycles and deflates during downcycles.

I don't want to own a shipyard for five years — I want to trade ships for five years if I'm being honest with myself, and I don't trade.

## Management Assessment — BELOW AVERAGE

I want management to be rational capital allocators who are candid about mistakes. The 18% share dilution over the last twelve months is not rational capital allocation at a cyclical peak. Issuing shares when your stock is near all-time highs to fund operations is the opposite of what Berkshire does. "When a company with mediocre economics issues shares at an elevated price, it's the existing shareholders who get diluted." And that's exactly what happened here.

They're selling the Gunsan shipyard to try and shore up the balance sheet — selling assets at the bottom of a down cycle is not what good management teams do. Good management teams preserve capital, sell assets at peaks, and buy back shares at troughs.

## Intrinsic Value Estimate — The Back of the Envelope

A business earning ₩2.4 trillion in owner earnings at the peak of a cycle, with no moat, high cyclicality, and mean reversion baked into its DNA, deserves a conservative multiple. I'd apply a 10x multiple to normalized owner earnings — and normalized earnings are likely 40-50% below peak cycle earnings.

Normalized Owner Earnings: approximately ₩1.2-1.4 trillion
Fair multiple: 10x (no moat, high cyclicality, capital-intensive)
**Fair value: ₩12-14 trillion market cap**

Current market cap: approximately ₩68 trillion (based on ₩667,000 share price × 102M shares after dilution).

That implies fair value per share of approximately **₩120,000-140,000**. Even on peak cycle earnings at 19.7x forward PE, the stock is overvalued. On normalized earnings — which is the only honest way to value a cyclical — this stock is worth a fraction of its current price.

Let me be conservative and use the GuruFocus estimate of ₩320,000-380,000 as fair value. Even that implies **43-52% downside** from ₩667,000.

## Margin of Safety

There is none. "Our favorite holding period is forever" only works when you're buying a wonderful business at a fair price. This is not a wonderful business, and the price is not fair. It is a capital-intensive, no-moat, high-cyclicality business that I wouldn't want to own for five minutes, let alone five years.

An investor buying at ₩667,000 is paying peak-cycle prices for a business that will inevitably face a down cycle. Mr. Market is offering HD Hyundai Heavy Industries at a price that bakes in the best-case scenario for LNG carrier demand, and that scenario is already failing.

## Bear Case — What Permanently Impairs the Moat

The moat is already absent, so the question is what prevents it from ever developing one: Chinese shipbuilding competition. Chinese yards are aggressively moving up the value chain into LNG carriers and high-value vessels. They have lower labor costs, government subsidies, and a massive domestic shipping market. If HD Hyundai Heavy loses its remaining pricing power in LNG carriers to Chinese competitors, there is no moat to fall back on — just a commodity shipbuilder with high fixed costs and a market that doesn't care about brand loyalty when prices are 15% lower across the street.

## Verdict

**AVOID**. There's no Margin of Safety, no Moat, and no Five-Year Test that this business passes. The stock market today is offering you a shipbuilder at the peak of its cycle, at a price that assumes the good times never end. And I've seen enough cycles in 60 years to know that the good times always end.

As I've said before: "The stock market is a device for transferring money from the impatient to the patient." At ₩667,000, HD Hyundai Heavy Industries is transferring money from the people who believe cycles have been repealed to the people who know they haven't.
