Let me be clear from the outset: I am not going to buy a single share of CRDO at $271.83, not today, not tomorrow, not next year. I don't care if it goes up 9% in a day or 90% in a month. ## Overview & Setup

When I look at a stock, I don't see a ticker. I see a business. I see people, products, customers, and competitive dynamics. I see cash flows, moats, and management. I do not see a chart that goes up and to the right, at least not primarily. I see a business that I might want to own for the next ten, twenty, or fifty years.

CRDO. I had to look it up. It is Credo Technology Group. They make high-speed connectivity solutions for data centers, artificial intelligence, and networking. They sell chips, optical engines, and other hardware that helps move data faster. The stock is up 9% today, and it is at an all-time high of $271.83. The market cap is $50.7 billion. The P/E is 108.7. The earnings per share is $2.50. The quarterly earnings growth is 362.2%. The beta is 3.229.

Now, let me tell you what catches my eye and what worries me.

What catches my eye is the growth. 362% earnings growth in a quarter is extraordinary. That tells me they are in the right place at the right time. The AI boom is real. Data centers are being built at a pace we have never seen. The demand for high-speed connectivity is exploding. If CRDO has a product that is essential to that buildout, they could have a wonderful business for a while.

What worries me is everything else. A P/E of 108.7 means you are paying 108 years of current earnings for the stock. That is not a valuation. That is a hope. A prayer. A story. And stories, as I have learned over 60 years, do not always end well.

I also worry about the beta of 3.229. That means this stock is three times more volatile than the market. When the market drops 10%, this stock drops 30%. When the market drops 50%, this stock drops 150%. That is not a business I want to own. That is a ticket to a roulette wheel.

And I worry about my circle of competence. I understand Coca-Cola. I understand American Express. I understand GEICO. I understand BNSF Railway. I understand Apple as a consumer products company. But Credo Technology? I am not sure I can explain in five minutes how they make money, who their customers are, and why they stay. That is a red flag. A big one.

As I said to CNBC in 2023, "I don't invest in what I do not understand." (https://buffett.cnbc.com)

So let me apply my Four Gates to CRDO. I suspect we will not get past the first one.

## Gate One: Circle of Competence

The test is simple: Can I explain in five minutes how this business makes money, who its customers are, and why they stay? If not, I do not invest.

Let me try.

Credo Technology Group makes high-speed connectivity solutions. They sell chips, optical engines, and line cards that help data centers move data faster. Their customers are the big cloud providers—Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta—and the networking equipment companies that supply them. They compete with companies like Broadcom, Marvell, and Intel.

The reason customers stay? Performance. If you are building a data center that costs $1 billion or more, you want the fastest, most reliable connectivity you can get. If CRDO's chips are 10% faster or 20% more power-efficient, that is a huge advantage. The switching costs are high because once you design a chip into your system, it is expensive to switch.

Okay. I can explain that. But can I really understand it?

The problem is that this is a technology business. And technology changes fast. What is state-of-the-art today is obsolete in three years. That is not a moat. That is a treadmill. And I do not invest in treadmills.

As I said about Apple, "It's a consumer company." (https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/31/cnbc-exclusive-transcript-berkshire-hathaway-chairman-warren-buffett-speaks-with-cnbcs-becky-quick-on-squawk-box-today.html)

Apple is a consumer products company. They sell phones and computers to people. I can understand why someone buys an iPhone. I cannot understand why someone buys a Credo chip. That is outside my circle.

I also think about the end market. CRDO's customers are hyperscale data center operators. That is a concentrated customer base. If Amazon decides to switch to a competitor, CRDO loses 20% of their revenue overnight. That is not a moat. That is a vulnerability.

So I am going to say something I rarely say: I do not understand this business well enough to invest in it. Gate One is a fail.

## Gate Two: Moat Analysis

Even if I could understand the business, I need to analyze the moat. What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?

Let me check the four types of moats:

**Cost Advantage:** Can CRDO produce or deliver cheaper than anyone else? I do not know. They are a fabless semiconductor company. They design chips, but they outsource manufacturing to TSMC or similar foundries. That means they do not have a cost advantage from manufacturing. They might have a design advantage, but that is intellectual property, not cost.

**Network Effect:** Does the product get more valuable the more it is used? Not really. A chip is a chip. It does not get better because more people use it. There is no network effect here.

**High Switching Costs:** This is the strongest argument for a moat. Once a data center operator designs a Credo chip into their system, it is expensive to switch. You have to redesign the system, retest everything, and risk downtime. That is a real switching cost. But is it durable? If a competitor comes out with a chip that is 20% faster and 30% cheaper, the switching cost becomes worth it.

**Intangible Assets:** Does the brand create pricing power? Credo is not a household name like Coca-Cola or American Express. Their brand is not a moat. It is a reputation among a small group of engineers.

So let me ask the moat question: "What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?"

The answer is: I am not sure. The technology cycle in semiconductors is 2-3 years, not 10+ years. Broadcom, Marvell, and Intel have deep pockets and large R&D budgets. They can and will catch up. The moat is narrow and temporary.

As I said about American Express, "They were getting a premium for American Express tickets... they can sell their traveler's check for more than Citigroup, Bank of America, Barclays, everybody had and they were getting a premium at the same time, everybody else was worried about them getting out of business." (https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/31/cnbc-exclusive-transcript-berkshire-hathaway-chairman-warren-buffett-speaks-with-cnbcs-becky-quick-on-squawk-box-today.html)

American Express had a moat that lasted decades. Credo has a moat that might last three years. That is not a moat. That is a window.

Gate Two is a fail.

## Gate Three: Owner Earnings & Intrinsic Value

Let me try to calculate the owner earnings.

The formula is: `Owner Earnings = Net Income + Depreciation/Amortization - Maintenance Capital Expenditures`

The P/E is 108.7, and the EPS is $2.50. That means net income is about $466 million ($2.50 * 186.3 million shares outstanding, approximately). But I need to check the balance sheet for depreciation and capex.

The problem is that I do not have the full financials in front of me. But I can make some assumptions.

For a fabless semiconductor company, depreciation is usually low because they do not own factories. But R&D is a real expense. In my framework, R&D is like maintenance capex for a technology company. You have to spend it every year just to stay in the same place.

So let me estimate. If CRDO has $466 million in net income, and they spend, say, $300 million on R&D (which is common for a company like this), then the real owner earnings might be much lower. In fact, they might be negative if you capitalize R&D as maintenance capex.

That is a problem. A business that needs to spend a huge portion of its earnings just to stay competitive is not a wonderful business. It is a treadmill.

Now let me look at the intrinsic value.

I use the long-term US Treasury yield as my discount rate. Today, that is around 4.5%. I also use a conservative growth assumption. If CRDO can grow earnings at 20% per year for the next 10 years (which is aggressive), and then 5% per year after that, the intrinsic value might be around $150 per share. But that is with very optimistic assumptions.

If I use a more conservative growth rate, say 15% for 5 years and then 3% forever, the intrinsic value drops to $80 per share. That is a far cry from $271.83.

The buy trigger is 50-60% of conservative intrinsic value. That means I would need to see a price of $40 to $48 per share before I even consider buying. We are 6 times that.

As I always say, "It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."

I am approximately right that CRDO is wildly overvalued. The numbers do not work. They do not come close.

Gate Three is a fail.

## Risk Assessment

Let me list the risks I see in CRDO.

**1. Valuation Risk:** The P/E of 108.7 is unsustainable. If earnings do not grow as expected, the stock could fall 50% or more. This is the biggest risk.

**2. Technology Risk:** The semiconductor industry is fast-moving. A competitor could come out with a better chip, and CRDO could become obsolete in 2-3 years.

**3. Customer Concentration Risk:** If CRDO's top 3 customers account for 70% of revenue, losing one could be catastrophic.

**4. Macro Risk:** If the AI boom slows down, demand for data center chips could collapse. This is a cyclical business, not a predictable one.

**5. Interest Rate Risk:** With the 10-year Treasury at 4.5%, the opportunity cost of owning a high-P/E stock is high. If rates go higher, the stock could fall further.

**6. Liquidity Risk:** The beta of 3.2 means this stock could drop 30% in a single day during a market crash. That is not a risk I want to take.

**7. Management Risk:** I do not know the management team. I do not know if they are rational capital allocators. I do not know if they will buy back stock at inflated prices or make foolish acquisitions.

**8. Dilution Risk:** A company with a P/E of 108 will be tempted to issue stock to fund acquisitions or employee compensation. That dilutes existing shareholders.

**9. Regulatory Risk:** The US government could impose restrictions on chip exports to China, which could hurt CRDO's revenue.

**10. Accounting Risk:** I do not have the full financial statements. I do not know if the earnings are real or if they are inflated by one-time items or stock-based compensation.

As I said in my 2026 interview, "You have this incredible cathedral called the American Economic System. Nobody's seen anything like it. I mean, it's the cathedral of all cathedrals.
