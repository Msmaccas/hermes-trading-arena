## GATE ONE: CIRCLE OF COMPETENCE

**The test:** Can you explain in five minutes how this business makes money, who its customers are, and why they stay?

I can explain Micron in five minutes. They make memory chips. They sell them to data centers, PC makers, smartphone manufacturers, and automotive companies. Their customers are large, powerful, and price-sensitive. There is no brand loyalty. If Samsung or SK Hynix offers a better price, the customer switches. The switching cost is zero. The product is a commodity.

So, does this pass the Circle of Competence test? **No.** Not for me. I do not understand the technology well enough to predict the next cycle. I do not understand when the next downturn will come. I do not understand how long the AI-driven demand will last. I am not a semiconductor analyst. I am a business analyst. And this business is outside my circle.

But let me be fair. I have made exceptions before. I bought Apple even though it was a technology company. But Apple had a moat. Micron does not have a moat. The moat is the key. Without a moat, you are buying a commodity producer in a cyclical industry. That is not my game.

> *“All I had to do was go out to Nebraska Furniture Mart and talk to customers.”*

Can I do that for Micron? No. I cannot walk into a store and ask a customer why they prefer Micron’s DRAM over Samsung’s. They don’t. They buy on price. The only reason they buy Micron is if Micron is cheaper or if they have a long-term contract. That is not a moat. That is a race to the bottom.

**Verdict on Gate One: FAIL.** I cannot explain this business in a way that gives me confidence for a 10-year hold.

## GATE TWO: MOAT ANALYSIS

**Four types of moat:**

| Type | Does Micron have it? |
|------|----------------------|
| **Cost Advantage** | Partially. Micron has scale, but so do Samsung and SK Hynix. The cost advantage is temporary. |
| **Network Effect** | No. Memory chips do not get more valuable as more people use them. |
| **High Switching Costs** | No. Customers switch on price. Contracts exist, but they are short-term. |
| **Intangible Assets** | No. Brand is irrelevant. Patents exist, but they are cross-licensed. |

Micron’s moat is weak. It is a cyclical commodity business. The only moat is the massive capital expenditure required to build a fab. That creates a barrier to entry, but it does not create pricing power. When demand falls, prices collapse. That is what happened in 2018-2019, when Micron’s stock fell from $60 to $30. That is what happened in 2022-2023, when the stock fell from $95 to $50. The cycle is brutal.

> *“What keeps competitors out for 10+ years?”*

For Micron, the answer is: nothing. The competitors are already in. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron are the big three. They all have scale. They all have access to capital. They all have the same technology. The only difference is execution. And execution can change overnight.

**Verdict on Gate Two: FAIL.** No durable competitive advantage.

## GATE THREE: OWNER EARNINGS & INTRINSIC VALUE

### Owner Earnings Formula

`Owner Earnings = Net Income + Depreciation/Amortization - Maintenance Capital Expenditures`

Let’s look at the numbers. Micron’s P/E is 53.5. That means the market is paying $53.50 for every $1 of earnings. That is expensive. But the EPS growth is 770.8% quarterly. That suggests that earnings are exploding. The question is: are these earnings sustainable?

Micron is a capital-intensive business. They must spend billions every year on new fabs. The depreciation is massive. The maintenance CapEx is huge. So the Owner Earnings are much lower than the reported Net Income. In fact, in many years, Micron’s Free Cash Flow is negative. They are spending more on CapEx than they are earning. That is not a wonderful business. That is a treadmill.

Let’s apply the **Retained Earnings Test**: For every dollar of retained earnings, has the market value increased by at least one dollar? Over the last 10 years, Micron has retained billions. The market value has gone up and down wildly. In 2018, the stock was $60. In 2020, it was $40. In 2024, it was $100. In 2025, it was $1,100. That is not a smooth increase. That is a roller coaster. The retained earnings test is inconclusive at best.

**Intrinsic Value:** I use the long-term US Treasury yield as the discount rate. Let’s say the risk-free rate is 4%. If Micron can earn $20 per share in a good year, the intrinsic value is $20 / 0.04 = $500 per share. But that assumes $20 is sustainable. In a bad year, earnings could be $5 or negative. So the intrinsic value is highly uncertain. It could be $100 or $1,000. That is not a range I am comfortable with.

> *“It is better to be approximately right than precisely wrong.”*

I am approximately right that this is a cyclical commodity business. I am precisely wrong if I try to value it at $1,133.99.

**Key Ratios:**
- ROE: Probably high in good years, but negative in bad years.
- Debt/Equity: Micron has debt, but it is manageable. However, they NEED debt to build fabs. That is a red flag.
- Capex/Net Income: This ratio is often >100%. That means they are spending more on CapEx than they earn. That is a terrible business model.
- Insider Ownership: Minimal. The management is not eating their own cooking.

**Verdict on Gate Three: FAIL.** The Owner Earnings are unreliable. The intrinsic value is unknowable. The capital intensity is a warning sign.
